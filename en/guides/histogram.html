How to Read an Image Histogram

A histogram compresses the tonal distribution of an image into one bar chart. Learn the three places to look — both ends and the middle — and you can judge an image before touching a single slider.

What the two axes mean

The horizontal axis is brightness: pure black (0) on the far left, pure white (255) on the far right, mid-grey in the centre. The vertical axis is the number of pixels sitting at that brightness. Millions of pixels get binned by brightness into one undulating curve — tall sections mean many pixels share that value, flat sections mean almost none do.

Colour images can usually switch to the individual R, G and B distributions. Similar curve shapes across the three channels mean little colour cast; one channel shifted right pulls the whole image toward that colour.

Three typical shapes

ShapeLooks likeMeans
Normal exposureMain peak centred, tails at both endsDetail in highlights and shadows — most room to grade
Underexposed (low key)Mass pushed leftWhether shadows are recoverable depends on whether the left edge has hit the wall
Overexposed (high key)Mass pushed rightAnything touching the right wall is dead white — unrecoverable
Double peakPeaks at both ends, dip in the middleTypical of backlit silhouettes or high-contrast scenes; little mid-tone data

No shape is inherently wrong — only mismatched with intent: a low-key night shot is fine, a low-key midday portrait is an accident.

The same shape means opposite things by genre: a large mass on the right is often the intended look for high-key portraiture but usually a mistake for still life, while an entirely left-shifted night scene is perfectly normal. Decide what the subject calls for before calling the distribution a failure.

Clipping lives at the ends

The most practical rule: look at both ends. A column pinned to the far left means pixels have hit pure black — that shadow detail is gone for good; the same at the right wall means burnt highlights. Lifting shadows later only yields flat grey noise, not information.

8-bit JPEG has only 256 levels per channel, so clipped values leave no margin; 16-bit TIFF has 65536 and often keeps usable data under the same apparent look. That is why shooting and intermediate exports prefer higher bit depth.

A histogram counts but never localises: it tells you what percentage of detail was lost, not where in the frame. Locating it still takes the preview and a sample readout — read the chart for the overall verdict, then return to the image to confirm the region. Far faster than eyeballing exposure back and forth.

What it helps you do

  1. Match exposure between two images: compare where the main peaks sit and bring them into the same range — consecutive frames then sit together;
  2. Judge contrast in AI output: one narrow clump in the middle = washed out; both ends pinned = crushed contrast;
  3. Vet incoming assets: reading the histogram beats guessing from a thumbnail.

FAQ

Does a tall peak mean overexposure?

No — position beats height. A tall peak only says many pixels share that value; it is clipping only when the pile sits against the far right wall.

What does one big centred peak mean?

Exposure concentrated around mid-grey with margin at both ends — the safest state. An extremely narrow peak, however, means a flat image that needs contrast.

Why does it differ from my editing app?

Different math: luminance-weighted (greener-sensitive) versus channel average, and some apply gamma first. Similar shape with different heights is normal — compare within one tool.

How do I read a depth map histogram?

The x-axis is millimetres: the main peak is where the scene's objects sit, zeros are invalid values counted separately, and a missing right side means the depth only covers nearby distances.
