Using a Cutout as a ControlNet Mask

The background remover gives you two PNGs: a transparent cutout and a black-and-white mask. Feed the wrong one and the model either ignores it or repaints the subject.

Three files that are easy to confuse

They all look like images. They carry different data, and swapping them is one of the most common mistakes in a ControlNet workflow.

FileWhat each pixel meansTypical use
Transparent PNG (cut)RGB is the original colour; alpha is the subject's opacityCompositing, a new background, image-to-image input
Grayscale maskGray value equals opacity: white keeps, black removesInpainting, regional control
Depth mapEach pixel is a distance, not an opacityDepth ControlNet, 3D reconstruction

A mask answers one question: is this pixel part of the subject? A depth map answers a different one: how far away is this surface? Send a mask to a depth model and black becomes near, white becomes far, and the output has nothing to do with your image.

Which direction does this tool's mask use?

The background remover writes the subject as white (255) and the background as black (0), with the whole image opaque. It comes straight from the model's alpha output, so the edge is a soft gradient rather than a hard binary cut.

That matches "keep the subject". Inpainting tools do not all agree. In A1111's inpaint mask, white usually means "repaint this area", which is the opposite of this file. Check the convention of the tool you are feeding before you connect anything. If it expects the inverse, invert the mask first.

ImageLab mask: white = keep subject, black = background
A1111 inpaint: white = repaint area, black = keep
→ usually invert before feeding inpaint

Cutout or mask?

Moving a person onto a new background is a job for the transparent PNG: use it as an image-to-image input or as a layer, and alpha decides the edge. Repainting one region while keeping the rest is a job for the grayscale mask, with the polarity checked first.

Depth ControlNet needs neither. It needs a single-channel distance map. The depth ControlNet guide covers the format; the cutout is supporting material, not the control image.

Choosing between the two models

For people, use MODNet at about 6.3 MB. It processes the long edge at 512 and handles hair, shoulders and semi-transparent edges better. For products, animals and architecture, use U2Netp at about 4.4 MB. It runs at a fixed 320 × 320, generalises more widely and produces coarser edges.

Both run one inference pass and upscale the result smoothly to the original size. Upscaling does not create edge detail: a 4K source still gets its mask from a 512 or 320 pass. This tool will not produce strand-level hair matting. That needs a dedicated matting model or manual work.

The first run downloads the inference engine (about 11 MB) and a model: 6.3 MB for MODNet, 4.4 MB for U2Netp. Running both once costs roughly 22 MB in total. The browser caches the files afterwards. Images stay local; inference runs in WASM and takes a few seconds depending on size and hardware.

Workflow

  1. Open the background remover and drop in an image. Choose MODNet for people, U2Netp for everything else.
  2. When inference finishes, inspect the transparent result. If part of the subject is missing or background survives, run the other model and compare.
  3. Export the mask on its own, or export the ZIP to get both -cut.png and -mask.png.
  4. Check the polarity in the target tool. Invert before use if needed.
  5. If mask and source differ in size, resize them to match first. Use nearest-neighbour or linear interpolation; avoid sharpening resizers on a mask.

Soft edges, fringes and jaggies

Mask edges are rarely 0 or 255. In-between values mean "this pixel may be half subject". That softness is useful for compositing and noisy for workflows that expect a binary mask. Threshold it (128 is a common starting point) if the tool only understands black and white.

A transparent PNG can show a white or black fringe in some editors. Transparent pixels still carry RGB, and resizing interpolates colour and alpha separately. Filling the transparent area with the destination background before scaling, or using the grayscale mask instead of alpha, avoids most of it.

White clothing against a white wall, glassware and smoke defeat every automatic cutout. Rather than cycling models, accept a rough mask and paint the edge by hand. Fixing a few pixels is cheaper than regenerating the image.

FAQ

Can I use the mask as a depth map?

No. A mask has foreground and background; a depth map has a continuous distance. A depth model will read the flat mask as a scene with near and far surfaces and produce an unrelated composition.

Why is the exported mask grey instead of black and white?

The model's alpha is continuous and the tool keeps that gradient. For a binary mask, apply a threshold (for example 128) in an image editor and convert to black and white.

Does removing the background shift colours?

The cutout's RGB comes from the original with no colour adjustment. It can look lighter because transparent areas are composited over a pale background.

Can it process a batch?

The page handles one image at a time. Once the model is loaded you can swap images without downloading again, but each image needs its own inference and export.
