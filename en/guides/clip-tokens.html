CLIP Tokens and the 75/77 Limit

You have seen both 75 and 77 quoted as the prompt limit. They are two readings of one constraint — here is the arithmetic, the segmentation rules, and how to verify your prompt segment by segment.

A token is not a word

The text encoder never reads words — it reads integers. Before anything else, the prompt passes through a BPE tokenizer: frequent combinations merge into large tokens, rare ones split into fragments. The CLIP vocabulary behind SD1.5 and SDXL holds only about 49,000 entries, and its merges match neither your keyboard's autocorrect nor an English word splitter — "masterpiece" may be one token or two, and only the merge table decides.

Two consequences follow. First, the same word counts differently across models — CLIP, T5 and GPT each own their vocabulary, so always count against the target model. Second, commas, spaces and weights all cost tokens — the brackets and digits in (word:1.4) are not free.

Where 75 and 77 come from

The CLIP text encoder accepts at most 77 positions, hard-coded in the model. One slot at the start holds the begin marker and one at the end holds the end marker, leaving 75 tokens for actual text — the two famous numbers are one sentence from two angles, not two competing standards.

BREAK and AND: two ways to segment

Long prompts are not truncated at 77 as a whole — they are encoded in batches. BREAK forces a new segment: each segment gets its own 77-token pass, and segments do not share attention. That is the direct tool when two concepts should each own half the frame, but more segments is not automatically better — the weaker the link between segments, the less earlier passages influence later ones.

AND blends two descriptions into one image with alternating weights, and most frontends encode each side as its own segment as well. Counting rules for AND vary between tools; this counter follows the common BREAK-segment convention, so count each side separately before relying on one total. For weight syntax itself see Prompt Weighting (a:b): Syntax and Tuning — its scope is bounded by segments too.

What happens on overflow

Content past 77 in a segment is truncated, not rejected — that is the first scene to inspect when something you wrote simply does not appear. Truncation cuts the tail, so order matters: subject words buried at the end are the first to go, while key concepts placed early or wrapped in their own bracket group keep their priority.

A subtler effect: weights apply only within their own segment. A strong word past position 75 sits across a segment boundary from the rest, so a "whole-sentence emphasis" quietly halves. Diagnose with the counter: if a red segment contains a key concept, move or split it — do not just raise the number.

Why Chinese costs double

The CLIP vocabulary is English-first: each Chinese character typically burns 1-2 tokens as BPE shatters it, while a common English word is often one token. The same description in Chinese can eat twice the budget and hit the wall sooner. Two practical rules: write prompts for SD in English and keep Chinese as notes; when you need a bilingual version, run it through prompt translate so nothing gets fragmented. The negative prompt follows exactly the same counting rules — see How to Write Effective Negative Prompts.

FAQ

What is the difference between 75 and 77?

Whether the two special tokens are counted: encoder length is 77, prompt-only budget is 75 — two readings of one limit.

Does exceeding 77 raise an error?

No. The tail of an over-long segment is silently truncated, so the last content disappears first — segment with BREAK or trim instead.

How many tokens is one Chinese character?

Typically 1-2, and badly fragmented. English prompts almost always cost less; keep Chinese as commentary.

Does T5 / SD3 also cap at 75?

No — T5-XXL allows 512 under SentencePiece. The counter lists the encodings, but its vocabulary is not bundled yet, so those numbers are estimates.
