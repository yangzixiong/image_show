Prompt Weighting (a:b): Syntax and Tuning

Weighting is the most-used control in a prompt, yet the many notations often produce the opposite of what you expected. This guide lays out the syntax, the arithmetic and how to tune it.

What a weight actually changes

Every sampling step pulls the image toward the positive prompt and away from the negative one, with CFG setting the overall pull. A weight changes something finer: the share of that pull belonging to one word or one bracketed group. The scope starts at the weight and ends at the next comma by default; parentheses carry their own boundary.

That explains the classic surprise: appending a weight to the end of a sentence only affects the last word. To strengthen a whole phrase, wrap that phrase in parentheses.

Five notations and their arithmetic

NotationMultiplierNotes
(word:1.4)×1.4Explicit weight — what you type is what you get; prefer this
(word)×1.1Shortcut, identical to (word:1.1)
((word))×1.21Nested multiplication: 1.1 × 1.1, not 1.2
[word]×0.9Bracket shortcut for (word:0.9)
(word:0.5)×0.5Explicit down-weight to suppress an element

Layers multiply: (((word))) is 1.1³ ≈ 1.33. Convert every shortcut to a decimal before multiplying and the arithmetic stays honest. Two advanced forms build on the same idea: AND blending (a|b) mixes two descriptions in one image, and the scheduled form (from:to:when) switches content across steps.

How much is too much

The practical range is 1.1 to 1.5. 1.1-1.2 nudges, 1.3-1.4 insists, and around 1.5 you start seeing oversaturation, singed edges and a painterly artefact look; beyond that the frame falls apart. A weight controls attention share, not effect strength — pushing past the threshold only squeezes the other words out.

Down-weighting mirrors this: 0.7-0.8 gently fades, 0.5 is already weak, below zero is meaningless. Weight syntax inside the negative prompt is identical — the only difference is direction: emphasis up front, stronger exclusion in the negative.

Keep the two control layers apart: CFG sets the overall pull, a weight only redistributes attention between words. A lone 2.0 at CFG 4 does almost nothing; CFG 12 paired with 2.0 blows the whole frame past the limit first. Set CFG where the model feels comfortable (roughly 5-8 for most), then nudge individual words with 1.1-1.4. Optimal values also drift across models and LoRAs — a LoRA trigger word often needs 1.2-1.3 to appear reliably while 1.1 suffices on the base model, so re-test from 1.1 when porting a prompt instead of copying old numbers.

Five common mistakes

  1. Full-width brackets: （word） is CJK punctuation and the parser ignores it — use ASCII ().
  2. Writing Chinese directly: the tokenizer is BPE-English-first, so Chinese fragments into inefficient tokens no weight can fix — translate first.
  3. Single-point weights diluted in long prompts: beyond 75 tokens the prompt splits and weights apply per segment; repeating a key phrase beats one huge number.
  4. Treating weight as a switch: to remove an element entirely, put it in the negative prompt instead of crushing the positive weight to 0.1.
  5. Confusing weight with CFG: when both are in play, set CFG for the overall look first and use weights for word-level allocation — reversed order only masks the real problem.

Verify it in ImageLab

Open the weight calculator, paste any notation — nested brackets expand to the effective multiplier, and composites like ((cat:1.5):1.2) resolve to a final number. Calculate before sampling and you will waste far fewer images. Check total length and segmentation with the token counter so your weighted words do not land in the second segment. Then paste the resolved multipliers back and verify by generating — weight behaviour is seed-dependent, so settle a version across two or three seeds.

FAQ

Are (word) and (word:1.1) exactly the same?

Yes — the shortcut is sugar for 1.1, and ((word)) equals 1.21 rather than 1.2 because nesting multiplies.

What is the maximum weight?

The syntax has no cap, but past 1.5 you mostly buy artefacts. If nothing seems to change, the cause is usually dilution or untranslated text, not too low a number.

Does the same syntax work in the negative prompt?

Identically — only the direction differs: (text:1.4) in the negative means "exclude text harder", not "want text more".

When should I use AND or BREAK?

AND (a|b) blends two mutually exclusive styles; BREAK forces segmentation so two concepts each own half the frame. Both cost tokens — measure with the token counter first.
